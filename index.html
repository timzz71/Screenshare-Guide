What is a screenshare?

A screenshare is simply a live video tour of your computer screen, guided by a staff member. They ask to see your screen to make sure everyone is playing fairly. It’s a routine check – nothing scary, and you always have the final say.

🔑 You must agree – it’s voluntary. You can stop at any time.

Why do staff ask for screenshares?

To keep the game fair for everyone. They want to confirm you’re not using any unauthorised programs that give you an unfair advantage. It’s not personal – it’s about protecting the community.

What will they check?

They’ll ask to see a few places on your PC. Here’s the simple version:

1
Running programs – they open Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc) to see what’s currently active.
2
Your Minecraft folder – they peek inside .minecraft, especially mods and versions folders.
3
Startup apps – they check what programs launch when Windows starts (just a quick list).
4
Recent files – a glance at Downloads or Desktop for any new, unfamiliar files.
5
Extra tools – sometimes they run a safe scanner that only reads files (it doesn’t install anything).

They will not look at your personal files (photos, documents, etc.) – only what’s relevant to the game.

How should you prepare?

  • Stay calm – it’s a normal procedure. Just follow the staff member’s instructions.
  • Be cooperative – answer questions honestly and open folders when asked.
  • Ask if you’re confused – they should explain everything in simple terms.
  • Close extra apps – it makes the process faster.
  • Have your voice chat ready – they’ll guide you through.

Your privacy & rights

  • Only share with trusted staff – verify they are official.
  • Share your screen, NOT control – they should never take over your mouse or keyboard.
  • You can say “stop” anytime – no questions asked, no penalties.
  • Recordings are kept private – only used for this check.
You’re always in control

Simple glossary of terms you might hear

Obfuscation
Code that’s made hard to read – sometimes used by cheat makers, but also by legitimate mods. It’s a clue, not proof.
Signature
A known pattern that detectors look for. A match can be a lead, but sometimes it’s a false alarm.
Hash
A unique fingerprint for a file. If two files have the same hash, they are identical.
Prefetch
A Windows record that shows a program ran and roughly when – helps build a timeline.
BAM
Another Windows record that tracks program activity – used to confirm other records.
Amcache
A history of file details, even if the file is now deleted – can show what was once on your PC.
USN Journal
A log of file changes (created, renamed, deleted) – helps explain missing files.
False positive
A warning that looks suspicious but has an innocent explanation – that’s why one tool result is never final.

Final tips for a smooth experience

  • If you’re unsure about a file, just ask – it might be harmless.
  • Remember that false alarms happen – a weird file doesn’t mean you’re guilty.
  • The staff are there to help, not to trick you. Be honest and it will be quick.
  • After the screenshare, you can go back to playing normally.
